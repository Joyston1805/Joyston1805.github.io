The finding came out of Excel. A packaging line was running 1.9 percentage points
above the 2% overpack buffer we allowed ourselves — every case going out the door
carried a little more product than it needed to, and nobody had a number for it
because nobody had ever put one there.

That part was straightforward. The hard part came after: turning a workbook only I
understood into something the operations team would open on a Monday morning without
me standing next to it explaining what the columns meant.

This is what I got wrong on the way there.

## Why this dashboard

The spreadsheet answered the question once. It couldn't answer it again next week.

Every time someone wanted an updated number, it meant a fresh export, a fresh set of
pivot tables, and a fresh chance for me to make a copy-paste error at 4pm. The
analysis wasn't the deliverable — a repeatable view of overpacking by line and by
product was.

The audience mattered more than I expected. This wasn't for analysts. It was for
people who would look at it for about twenty seconds, standing up, possibly on a
phone, wanting one thing: *is anything running hot right now, and where.* Every
design decision downstream came from that sentence.

## Data modeling

I started the way I think most people start: one flat table, dumped straight out of
the production system, and every field I might conceivably need glued onto it.

It worked until I added a second table. Then the slicers stopped filtering things
they obviously should have filtered, one of my totals started double-counting, and I
spent an evening convinced Power BI was broken before accepting that the problem was
the shape of my data.

What I eventually landed on was a star schema:

- **A fact table** — one row per production run, with the numeric columns only:
  target weight, actual weight, units, scrap.
- **Dimension tables** — Product, Line, Shift, and a proper Date table, each
  connected to the fact table one-to-many.

Three things I'd underline for anyone about to do this for the first time:

**Build the date table before you build anything else.** Not the date column that
came with your data — a separate table with one row per day, marked with *Mark as
Date Table*. Half the time intelligence functions in DAX quietly misbehave without
it, and they misbehave in ways that look like your numbers are just slightly wrong
rather than obviously broken. Those are the worst bugs to find.

**Clean it in Power Query, not in the report.** My first instinct was to fix the
messy product names with a calculated column. That works, and then it sits there
invisibly forever. Doing it in Power Query means the transformation is documented,
repeatable, and visible to whoever inherits the file.

**The source data was messier than the analysis let on.** Inconsistent SKU naming
across shifts, a handful of runs with no line recorded, dates stored as text. The
modelling step is where you find out how much you were unconsciously smoothing over
in Excel.

## The DAX that mattered

None of the measures here are clever. The lesson was in the failures around them.

The core calculation:

```dax
Overpack % =
DIVIDE(
    SUM( Production[Actual Weight] ) - SUM( Production[Target Weight] ),
    SUM( Production[Target Weight] )
)
```

And the one people actually cared about — how far past the allowance we were:

```dax
Excess Above Buffer =
[Overpack %] - 0.02
```

Three things I learned the hard way:

**`DIVIDE()` instead of `/`.** The first version of that measure used a slash. It
worked fine until a product filtered down to a period with no production, the
denominator hit zero, and the card showed an error instead of a blank. `DIVIDE()`
handles that natively and takes an optional third argument for what to return
instead.

**Measures, not calculated columns.** I wrote `Overpack %` as a calculated column
first, computing it row by row. It gave the right answer per row and the wrong answer
everywhere else, because averaging a column of percentages is not the same as
dividing the totals. A run of 40 units and a run of 4,000 units are not equally
important, and a calculated column treats them as though they are. This was the
single biggest conceptual jump for me: a measure is evaluated in whatever context
you drop it into, and that's the whole point of it.

**Write the aggregation before the visual.** I kept dragging fields onto a chart and
hoping. Deciding explicitly what the number *means* first — "weighted overpack across
whatever the user has filtered to" — made the DAX obvious.

## Design decisions

The first version had nine visuals, a gauge, and a pie chart. It was unusable.

What survived:

- **Three KPI cards across the top** — overall overpack %, excess above buffer, and
  estimated material cost of that excess. Someone should get the headline in about
  three seconds without touching anything.
- **One horizontal bar chart** — overpack % by line, sorted descending, so the worst
  offender is always at the top left where the eye lands first.
- **One trend line** — the same metric over time, to distinguish "bad week" from
  "ongoing problem."
- **Two slicers** — date range and line. That's it.

What I cut, and why:

The **pie chart** went because comparing wedge sizes is hard and comparing bar
lengths is easy. The **gauge** went because it took up a quarter of the canvas to
show one number that a card showed better. About **five other charts** went because
they were interesting to me and irrelevant to the decision the report existed to
support.

On colour: I used one accent, applied only to values above the 2% buffer. Everything
else stayed neutral. When only the problem is coloured, the problem is the thing you
see. I'd originally given every line its own colour, which meant the chart looked
busy and communicated nothing.

## What I'd change next time

**I'd ask what decision this drives before I built anything.** I built a dashboard
that reported overpacking accurately, then discovered the more useful question was
which *products* were most sensitive to it — a slightly different model. Two
questions at the start would have saved a rebuild.

**I'd validate against the source earlier.** I checked my totals against the raw
export at the end, when a mismatch would have meant rework. Checking after each
modelling step is less satisfying and much cheaper.

**I'd document the measures inside the file.** Six weeks later I couldn't remember
why one of them excluded a particular shift. Description fields exist. I now use
them.

**I'd think about handover from day one.** The real test of a dashboard isn't whether
it answers the question. It's whether it still answers the question when the person
who built it isn't in the building.

The Excel analysis found the 1.9%. The dashboard is what made it something the
business could keep watching — and building it taught me that the modelling and the
layout are not the boring parts around the analysis. Most of the time, they *are*
the analysis.
